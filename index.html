Cross-FM: A Cross-Species Foundation Model for
Intracortical Brain–Computer Interfaces

A neural foundation model — manifold-constrained hyper-connections + sparse mixture-of-experts, self-supervised across heterogeneous recording pools — evaluated on human speech (T12), human handwriting (H2), and non-human primate reaching (Flint) with real multi-seed statistics.

Abstract

Building a single neural network that transfers usefully across species, tasks, and recording hardware is a central open problem for intracortical brain–computer interfaces (iBCIs). We present Cross-FM, a compact encoder that combines manifold-constrained hyper-connections (mHC) in place of standard residual connections with a sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE) feed-forward sublayer, pretrained via masked reconstruction across five heterogeneous neural-data pools. We evaluate transfer on three held-out downstream benchmarks spanning two species and three behaviors — human attempted-speech decoding (T12), human single-letter handwriting classification (H2), and non-human-primate center-out reaching (Flint) — at three model scales (6M–45M parameters), reporting results as genuine mean ± SD over four random seeds rather than single-run point estimates, across five pretraining pools and three model scales (45 pool×size conditions per benchmark). We find that a tokenization-aware fine-tuning recipe is decisive: naively frozen transfer underperforms training from scratch, while unfreezing the full model from step one flips the comparison entirely — pretraining beats scratch training with non-overlapping seed variance in all 45 pool×size conditions on every benchmark. No single pretraining pool is universally best, however: the strongest pool changes with model size and benchmark. A controlled architecture ablation further shows that neither mHC nor MoE is universally beneficial — each component's contribution is task-dependent, arguing against a single fixed "best" architecture for cross-species iBCI transfer.

Method

Cross-FM tokenizes raw multi-channel neural activity into fixed-width patches, encodes them with a stack of blocks that route each sublayer's residual connection through a Sinkhorn-constrained hyper-connection instead of a plain skip, and pretrains the whole stack with a masked-reconstruction objective across five pretraining pools (A–E).

Architecture Ablation

Two controlled variants, evaluated identically (6M, pool E, 100% data, 4 seeds): removing mHC entirely (residual_moe, plain residual connections, MoE-FFN kept) and keeping mHC while replacing the sparse MoE-FFN with a single dense FFN (mhc_dense).

No single architecture wins everywhere. H2 is indistinguishable across all three variants once real seed variance is accounted for. On T12, dropping mHC is a genuine, reproducible improvement over the full model. On Flint, both ablations beat the full baseline outright — MoE looks actively unhelpful for this benchmark specifically.

Key Findings

Fine-tuning recipe > architecture, at first glance. The single largest lever in this study wasn't a model change — it was replacing a frozen/gradual-unfreeze transfer recipe with a conventional one (whole model trainable from step one). The same checkpoints went from losing to scratch training on every benchmark, to beating it, purely from that recipe change.
Tokenization must match the task's timescale. Native 10 ms tokenization (patch_size=1) roughly doubled H2 handwriting accuracy over coarse 320 ms patches, but hurt long-sequence CTC decoding on small data. Overlapping-window tokenization (dense output, wide per-token context) was the decisive fix for T12 speech decoding instead.
Single-seed point estimates can mislead. T12's baseline showed 6× the variance (SD 0.015) of either architecture ablation (SD 0.002–0.004) at 6M — a single run there would have been a poor guide to which architecture is actually better.
No pretraining pool is universally best. Across the full 45-cell pool×size sweep, pool C is the most consistently strong (top-2 on nearly every benchmark/size) and pool B the most consistently weak, but the single best pool for a given benchmark still changes with model size — e.g. Flint's best pool moves D (10M)→A (45M). Every pool beats Scratch everywhere, with zero exceptions across all 45 cells.
